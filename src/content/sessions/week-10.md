An audit is only as good as its willingness to be surprised by the data.
This lab gives you the Office's real release and asks you to read it
carefully before concluding anything.

## Before the lab

Reread your week 6 defence of whichever policy you chose, including your
note on how the Priority Lane's self-declared urgency could be misused.

## In the lab

> **Office of Everything file — synthetic case material.** Released usage
> summary, three months, Priority Lane declarations by request category:
> "dispute a printer credit" 4%, "collect a form" 61%, "enrolment variation"
> 30%, other 5%. The lane's stated purpose at launch was "for the small
> number of matters that are genuinely time-critical."

State plainly what the data shows: which category dominates Priority Lane
use, and whether that matches a plausible reading of "genuinely
time-critical." Resist finishing this in one sentence — check whether there
is an innocent explanation (for instance, whether "collect a form" ever
has a genuinely urgent version) before concluding the lane is being
overused.

Write your conclusion as a claim the data actually supports: not "people are
abusing the Priority Lane," but a specific statement of what proportion of
declared urgency goes to which category, and what that implies for whether
the lane, as currently specified, is measuring urgency or something else.

## Afterwards

Keep this audit. Week 12's redesign of the Office of Everything has to
either defend the Priority Lane as it stands, given this data, or change it
— and either choice needs to cite this week's finding, not simply repeat
week 6's original defence. Week 11 asks what happens to every rule
considered so far on the office's worst day.
