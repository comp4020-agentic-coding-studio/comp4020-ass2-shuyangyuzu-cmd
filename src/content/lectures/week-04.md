For three weeks the Office of Everything has run on one printed rule: take a
number, numbers are served in order. This lecture stops measuring that rule
and starts asking whether it is a good one — the course's first move from
"choose who goes next" rather than "measure the wait."

## Outline

- first come, first served (FCFS) as a policy, not a default: it is one
  ordering choice among several, and it was chosen, even if nobody remembers
  choosing it
- what FCFS rewards: arrival time, and only arrival time — it ignores how
  long a request will take to serve and how urgent it is
- what "fair" could mean here: equal treatment of arrival order is one kind
  of fairness; it is not the same as equal treatment of outcomes, and the two
  can point in different directions
- the case against changing it: FCFS is simple to explain, simple to audit,
  and hard to game — a real cost of any alternative this course considers
  from here on
- where this leaves the Office: the front counter serves both a two-minute
  "collect a form" request and a twenty-minute "dispute a printer credit"
  request in the same single-file line, on principle

> **Office of Everything file — synthetic case material.** A students'
> association complaint, logged this week: "I was ticket 41. The people
> behind me with quick questions waited as long as I did with a complicated
> one. Why does a simple case wait behind a complicated one just because it
> arrived second?"

The Queue Lab asks you to take the complaint seriously on FCFS's own terms —
figure out exactly what it is asking the Office to change, and what that
change would cost the people FCFS currently protects.
