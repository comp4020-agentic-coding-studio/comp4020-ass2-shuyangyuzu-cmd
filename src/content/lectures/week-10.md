The Priority Lane was introduced in week 6 on the strength of its stated
purpose: urgent matters served first, urgency self-declared in good faith.
This lecture starts the course's final movement — defending a waiting
system — by auditing that lane against what actually happened once it
opened, not what it was meant to do.

## Outline

- the gap between a policy's stated purpose and its measured effect: this
  course has built the tools to close that gap (weeks 2–3's statistics,
  week 6's comparison method) and this week applies them to the Office's
  own choice
- an audit needs a release of real usage data to check against — the Office
  of Everything's is presented today for the first time, deliberately, as
  the object of this week's Queue Lab
- what "who benefits" means precisely here: which category of requester
  uses the Priority Lane, how often, and how that compares to who the lane
  was designed for
- self-declared urgency's incentive problem (raised in week 6) checked
  against evidence rather than argued from first principles
- an audit is not automatically a condemnation: a rule can be used more than
  intended and still be defensible, or used exactly as intended and still be
  worth reconsidering — the Queue Lab asks you to conclude from the data, not
  from the fact that an audit was requested

> **Office of Everything file — synthetic case material.** A student
> Freedom-of-Information-style request, granted this week: three months of
> anonymised Priority Lane usage, showing which request categories declared
> urgency and how often.

The Queue Lab gives you that release and asks what it actually shows about
who the lane has been serving, ahead of week 12's full redesign.
